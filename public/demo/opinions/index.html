Atlas — unstructured opinion agent

Overview

When a public company is sold, its board hires an investment bank to answer one question in writing: "Is the price fair to the shareholders?" The resulting documents, fairness opinions, are among the most litigated documents a bank produces. When a deal is challenged, the questions are always about the record: what exactly did the opinion cover, what did comparable opinions assume, and so on.

Each deal leaves up to three public documents behind: the opinion letter itself, the description of the analysis filed with the merger proxy, and any supplemental disclosures filed afterward. This corpus holds 583 of them across 223 deals, 2007–2026.

Today, consulting the record means someone manually digging through hundreds of filings. Interest in improving this process comes from the top of the firm: senior leadership wants to be able to ask arbitrary questions of these documents, and obtain answers on the spot.

A structured assistant already exists, capable of answering questions with column-shaped answers (counts, breakdowns) from a curated dataset. But the answers to many questions that C-suite might ask live in prose, not columns: what an opinion said, how it was worded, what assumptions comparable deals used. Answering those requires working from the raw filings themselves.

The existing unstructured assistant is a model with the documents stuffed into its context window. This fails for several reasons:

  • It can't see the full corpus: a context window fits only a few dozen opinions out of 583.
  • It has no tools. Computations occur inside the model's latent space, which works for simple arithmetic but fails for anything harder.
  • It can't guarantee completeness: "average over X" is computed over whatever was retrieved, which (for the above reasons) is non-deterministic.

The idea behind this unstructured assistant, Atlas, is to replace the single context window with an agent equipped with three simple tools: document-level embeddings for precedents and outliers, chunk-level embeddings for assumptions and wording, and an exhaustive scan for aggregates.

Architecture

One question, one orchestrating model, three routes: the agent picks whichever route can guarantee what the question needs, and every claim in the answer carries a citation back to a filing and page.

Embeddings

Every document is cut into passages of roughly 600 tokens, and every passage gets a vector. That one pass yields two layers: passage vectors for finding where something is said, and document vectors — built from each document's passages — for finding which documents resemble each other. The two layers answer different questions and power different tools.

Document level — "which opinions are like this one?"

One point per document. This is the layer for precedent questions ("what's the precedent set for a conflicts-committee buy-in?") and for outliers ("which letters are worded least like the rest?") — questions that are natively about whole opinions, where working from passages would just mean re-aggregating back up to documents.

Two complementary similarity signals are averaged. A lexical signal scores documents by their distinctive vocabulary. A semantic signal (neural embeddings pooled from passage vectors) captures meaning — but pooled vectors share a large "this is a fairness opinion" component that makes every document look alike, so the shared directions are removed before use. Measured on a 143-query benchmark over six known document families, the hybrid beats either signal alone (macro precision@5 0.67 vs 0.65 semantic-only, 0.55 lexical-only).

Chunk level — "where does it say that?"

Passages are cut at natural paragraph boundaries with their section headings attached, and the 1,067 tables in the filings are kept as their own typed chunks — a DCF assumptions table should be findable as a table. 9,588 chunks in total, each carrying deal, document kind, year, and page, so every search hit is citable to the page.

Search is hybrid: the meaning vector finds paraphrases ("terminal value assumptions" finds the perpetuity-growth discussion), while a keyword channel protects exact tokens — tickers, "8point3", dollar amounts — that meaning vectors blur. Near-identical boilerplate shared across deals is down-weighted so it doesn't crowd out deal-specific passages, but stays reachable when the question is about the standard language itself.

On eval questions with a known ground-truth passage, the right passage lands in the top five 9 times out of 9, and at rank one in 7 of 9.

These layers power three of the agent's tools: find_similar (precedents and outliers), search_passages (passage retrieval with citations), and read_document (exact pages, once search has located the spot).

Exhaustive scan

Search has a structural limit: it returns the best few matches, and can never promise there wasn't an eleventh document it missed. Some questions are exactly about completeness — "the average discount rate across healthcare deals since 2024" is wrong if even one matching document is skipped. Those questions get their own tool.

scan_documents runs in two steps. A deterministic filter picks the universe (year, document kind, deal — exact metadata, no model involved). Then every document in the universe is read, in one of two modes:

  • regex — free and exact: pattern counts per document, with sample snippets. Example: counting opinion letters addressed to a conflicts committee by scanning each letter's addressee block.
  • extract — a cheap model (claude-haiku-4.5) reads each document and returns exactly the requested JSON fields, including a relevance flag so loosely-filtered documents are rejected by reading them, not by guessing from names. Roughly $0.01 per document.

Aggregation is never done by a model: numeric fields get min/max/mean/median computed in code, and any further arithmetic goes through calc, a guarded calculator. The orchestrator is forbidden from doing mental math over lists of numbers.

End to end, "what discount rates did healthcare DCF analyses use in the past two years?" becomes: filter to opinion descriptions from 2024 on → the reader model flags which are healthcare and extracts each discount-rate range → statistics computed in code → a range, a mean, and a per-deal table with citations.

The agent

The orchestrator is claude-opus-4-8 with six tools and hard rules. The design principle, inherited from the structured assistant: definitions live in tools, where they are pinned and testable — the model routes, reads, and synthesizes with citations, and never invents a corpus fact.

ToolAnswersGuarantee
list_documentshow many, which deals, breakdowns by adviser / year / kindexact counts over the manifest; free
search_passages"where does a document discuss X"best-matching passages with page citations; never used to count
read_documentthe exact wording of specific pagesverbatim filing text
find_similarprecedents ("opinions like X") and outliersa ranking over every document in the volume
scan_documentscount or extract something from every matching documentcompleteness by construction; regex free, reader ~$0.01/doc
calcarithmetic over tool outputsdeterministic; the model never does mental math

The rules that do the most work:

  • Counts never come from search — top-k retrieval is not exhaustive, so anything shaped like "how many" or "all deals that…" must go through the manifest or a scan.
  • Every factual claim carries a citation, [Target ~ Acquirer, doc-kind, p.N], checkable against the filing in seconds.
  • Every count states its operationalization: which field, which pattern, which universe.
  • Scope precision: what an opinion expressly does not address is reported as carefully as what it does — a fairness conclusion is never extended to parties the letter excludes.
  • Abstention is scoped: if the corpus can't support part of an answer, the agent names the missing piece and answers the rest. Asked about a deal that isn't in the corpus, it says so rather than summarizing from memory.

Evaluated on a 12-question set spanning single-document lookups, metadata aggregates, text-scan counts, precedent retrieval, and two traps (a scope trap and an absent-document trap): 12/12 — including one case where the agent corrected the answer key, finding a growth-rate disclosure under a terminology variant the key's detector had missed, with the correct attribution. The honest caveat: the eval was authored alongside the system; the next round of questions should come from its users.

Cost per question: metadata and lookups $0.04–0.07, outlier explanations ~$0.15, a full reader scan ~$0.40.
